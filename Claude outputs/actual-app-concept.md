# Actual (codename)

## What it is

Actual is a personal time estimation app. It learns the gap between what you think something will take and what it actually takes, and it quietly recalibrates your future estimates based on your own history, not a generic average pulled from other people.

The idea comes from a well documented human bias called the planning fallacy, people are consistently optimistic about how long things will take, even when they've been wrong about the exact same kind of task many times before. Most tools that touch this problem, time trackers, budgeting apps, project management software, will show you a chart of estimated versus actual after the fact and leave you to do the math yourself. Actual does the math for you, continuously, and hands you back a corrected number the next time you plan something similar.

The app does not judge how anyone spends their time. It measures. Whether a gap is spent working, commuting, calling a parent, or scrolling a phone, the app records what happened honestly and lets the person decide for themselves whether that is fine or something they would rather change.

## Why now, why this angle

Commute time, traffic, and public transport booking are already solved problems, owned by Google Maps, Citymapper, and similar players with years of infrastructure and city level API deals behind them. Trying to compete there directly would mean fighting giants over a commodity feature.

The one thing none of them do is learn an individual person's own bias and correct for it automatically. A close relative of this idea exists in a small side project called EstimateLab, which computes a personal bias factor from logged tasks, but it stops at showing the number and expects the person to self correct. Time trackers like Clockify and Toggl show estimated versus actual in reports but do not compute a personal multiplier or feed it back into the next estimate. Waze is the closest proof that this kind of per person historical learning works at scale, it personalizes driving ETAs from an individual's own past trips, but it solves a driving speed problem, not a planning bias problem.

That gap, automatic personal recalibration, applied broadly across commutes and everyday tasks, is open. This is the wedge worth building around.

## Positioning

Tagline: know your time.

The name and tagline are meant to describe a feeling, not a mechanism. The app is not selling traffic data or ticket buying, those are commodity features borrowed from maps APIs in the background. It is selling self awareness, an honest, ongoing record of how someone's own estimates compare to their own reality, across both errands and commutes, in one place instead of split across a time tracker, a maps app, and a to do list.

## Core features

**Estimate capture.** Whenever someone adds a task or a trip, the app asks briefly how long they think it will take. This has to stay nearly frictionless, a single number or a quick slider, since any added effort here means people stop doing it within days and the whole system loses its data.

*Algorithm, staged rollout:*

Stage 1, raw input. On task or trip creation, prompt for estimated_duration. Input methods: numeric field, or rounded presets (15, 30, 60, 90 minutes, custom). If no input given, set estimated_duration = null, proceed without blocking. Store estimated_duration alongside category_id and context_tag.

Stage 2, multi-channel normalization. Accept input from three channels: manual tap, voice parse, widget quick-select. Each channel maps to the same internal schema: {category_id, context_tag, estimated_duration_minutes}. Voice parser extracts duration and category from natural phrase, normalizes to integer minutes, discards phrasing after extraction. Reject or flag inputs that fail to parse into a valid category or duration, fall back to manual entry.

Stage 3, conditional pre-fill. For each category_id and context_tag pair, maintain instance_count. If instance_count is below a minimum threshold (default 5 to 7), estimated_duration field stays blank, user enters raw guess. If instance_count meets the threshold, pre-fill estimated_duration with the recalibrated_estimate from the bias engine, user can accept in one tap or override by editing the value. Log whether the final estimate matches the pre-filled value unmodified, or was user-overridden, since override rate is itself a useful signal.

Stage 4, weighted recalculation. recalibrated_estimate is a weighted average of actual_durations, not a flat average. Weight function is recency-weighted, the most recent 10 to 15 instances count more than older ones, using linear or exponential decay decided at implementation. Recalculate on each new actual_duration write, not on a fixed schedule. Split the calculation per category_id and context_tag pair, never blended across contexts.

Stage 5, confidence signal. confidence_level is a function of instance_count, tiered as low (5 to 15), medium (15 to 40), high (40+). Display confidence_level as a visual weight or instance count adjacent to the pre-filled number at capture time, not only in the insight view. No numeric estimate is shown without its paired confidence_level once the minimum threshold is met.

Shipping order: stage 1 and 2 form the minimum viable version, capture works, nothing learned yet. Stage 3 is the first point where the core product promise activates. Stages 4 and 5 are trust and accuracy refinements, needed before wide launch but not before internal testing.

**Actual outcome tracking.** For trips, this happens automatically through GPS and maps, the app can see when someone left and arrived and compute the real duration without asking. For tasks, it relies on a simple start and end log. There is no approval or override step, the actual outcome feeds straight into the person's running average the moment it is recorded.

*Algorithm, staged rollout:*

Stage 1, session lifecycle. Every task or trip becomes a session object the moment it's created: session_id, category_id, context_tag, estimated_duration, start_time, end_time, actual_duration. start_time is set the moment the person actively begins the session, either by voice, widget tap, or manual start. actual_duration stays null until the session closes.

Stage 2, closing method by category type. Two closing paths, branching on category type. For trip categories, start_time is set on detected departure via GPS movement past a location threshold, end_time is set on detected arrival at the destination, arrival defined as GPS position stable within a small radius for a minimum dwell time, for example two minutes, to avoid false positives at a red light or a brief stop. actual_duration is end_time minus start_time, computed automatically, no user action required. For task categories, no location signal exists, so start_time and end_time both depend on explicit user action, voice command, widget tap, or manual button. No automatic inference is attempted for non location tasks in this version, since guessing when a task like a work session truly began or ended without an external signal is unreliable and risks silently corrupting the data the bias engine depends on.

Stage 3, no correction step. On session close, actual_duration is computed and written immediately. No confirmation prompt, no was this correct step, no undo flow beyond a basic edit before close window. This preserves the neutral, observational design agreed earlier, the system is not asking for agreement, it is recording what happened.

Stage 4, write to bias engine input. Once a session closes with both estimated_duration and actual_duration populated, it becomes one instance in that category and context tag's dataset, feeding directly into the weighted recalculation described in the estimate capture algorithm. A session with a null estimated_duration, meaning the person skipped the guess, still writes actual_duration, contributing to raw duration data even without contributing an estimate to compare against.

Stage 5, edge case handling. Define explicit rules for a small set of predictable failure states. A trip that never resolves an arrival, GPS lost or phone died, auto closes after a maximum duration ceiling per category, configurable, default high enough not to cut off a legitimately long session, and gets flagged low confidence rather than silently treated as normal data. A task manually started but never manually ended is auto closed after a similar ceiling and flagged the same way. Flagged sessions are excluded from the bias engine's weighted average by default, visible in the insight view as an outlier rather than deleted outright, so the person can see and correct it if they choose.

Shipping order: stage 1 and 2 for trip categories form the first buildable slice, since GPS based auto tracking is the most differentiated and lowest friction part of the feature. Task category manual start and stop can ship in parallel since it reuses the same session object. Stage 3 is a design constraint present from day one, not a later addition. Stage 4 activates the moment stage 1 of estimate capture also exists. Stage 5 can follow shortly after initial release, once real usage shows what the actual common failure patterns look like.

**Personal bias engine.** The core of the product. Every pair of estimate and actual outcome builds a running multiplier for that person, broken down by category rather than a single number for their whole life, since someone might run ten percent late on commutes and eighty percent late on answering emails. The engine needs a handful of logged instances per category before it starts adjusting anything, and it keeps updating quietly as more data comes in.

*Should this use AI.* The core calculation does not need it and should not use it. A weighted ratio between estimate and actual, with recency decay, is plain statistics, it runs instantly, costs almost nothing, and stays fully explainable. That explainability is not a nice to have, it's the whole product's credibility, if a person asks why the app expects a task to take forty minutes instead of twenty, the honest answer has to be that their last twelve similar sessions averaged forty, not that a model predicted it. A black box sitting under a product whose entire pitch is telling people the truth about their own numbers would quietly undercut the pitch itself. Where AI does earn its place is at the edges, not the center, parsing messy natural speech in voice input, judging what counts as a meaningful pattern shift in drift detection rather than one fixed threshold for everyone, and later, matching a free window to a worthwhile suggestion, since that's a fuzzy judgment call rather than a clean number. The center of the product should stay a plain, fast, explainable engine, with AI used narrowly wherever the input is language or judgment rather than a measurable duration.

*Algorithm, staged rollout:*

Stage 1, data shape. The engine reads from the same session objects actual outcome tracking writes, filtered to instances where both estimated_duration and actual_duration are non null, and not flagged low_confidence. Each instance is keyed by category_id and context_tag together, never by category alone, so normal work sessions and high pressure work sessions build entirely separate datasets from the start.

Stage 2, the core ratio. For each instance, compute a bias_ratio equal to actual_duration divided by estimated_duration. A ratio above 1 means the person underestimated, below 1 means they overestimated, exactly 1 means they were spot on. This ratio, not a raw time difference, is the unit the whole engine works with, since a ratio scales correctly whether the task is five minutes or five hours, while a flat difference like plus ten minutes does not mean the same thing across both.

Stage 3, weighted aggregation. The recalibrated_multiplier for a given category and context tag equals a weighted average of bias_ratio across that dataset's instances, weight decaying by recency, the most recent 10 to 15 instances weighted higher than older ones. This is recomputed every time a new instance is written, not on a timer, since the point is staying current with the person's latest pattern rather than running on a stale nightly batch.

Stage 4, applying the multiplier. When estimate capture asks for a pre-fill, the value shown is the raw guess the person typed multiplied by the recalibrated_multiplier, if they've already entered a guess, or the recent average actual duration directly, if no raw guess exists yet and the app is pre-filling blind based on history alone. Both paths are needed, since sometimes a person types a guess first and the app adjusts it, and sometimes the app suggests a number before they've guessed anything at all.

Stage 5, category and context isolation with fallback. If a specific category and context tag pair has fewer instances than the minimum threshold, but the parent category has enough instances across all context tags combined, the engine falls back to the category level multiplier rather than showing nothing, clearly marked as a broader estimate rather than a precise one. This prevents a brand new context tag from sitting through a cold start with zero guidance, while the more specific number remains the eventual target once enough data exists for it.

Stage 6, drift detection. The recalibrated_multiplier is tracked over time as its own small series per category and context tag. If it shifts meaningfully within a short window, for example moving by more than roughly 20 percent across the last 10 instances compared to the 10 before that, this gets flagged as a life pattern change rather than noise, worth surfacing gently in the insight view, since a real shift, a new job, a new commute, a new baby, deserves the person's attention rather than being silently absorbed into one long running number.

Stage 7, output object. The engine's output for any category and context tag pair is never a bare number, it's a structured object holding the recalibrated_multiplier, the instance_count, the confidence_level, and the drift_flag. Every other feature that touches an estimate, capture, display, insight view, nudges, consumes this same object rather than reimplementing its own version of the calculation.

Shipping order: stages 1 through 4 form the actual minimum viable engine, nothing in the product works without them. Stage 5 fallback logic should ship alongside, since cold start on every new context tag is a real and immediate problem, not an edge case to defer. Stages 6 and 7 as a formal structured object can follow shortly after, once enough real usage data exists to confirm the drift threshold is tuned sensibly rather than guessed.

**Recalibrated estimate display.** When someone enters a guess, the app shows an adjusted number next to it, based on their own history, along with how many past instances that adjustment rests on and the overall trend. A number built on two data points should not look as confident as one built on forty, and the display should make that difference visible rather than presenting every estimate with the same false authority.

*Algorithm, staged rollout:*

Stage 1, trigger conditions. The display activates any time a category and context tag pair has an existing bias engine output, whether the person is actively entering a raw guess right now, or just glancing at a task before deciding whether to start it. It appears not only at the moment of estimate capture, but also ahead of time, for instance the night before while looking at tomorrow's calendar, so the person can plan around a realistic number rather than only discovering it the moment they commit to starting.

Stage 2, dual number layout. When a raw_input_guess exists, both numbers render together, the person's own guess and the recalibrated_estimate, never one replacing the other silently. The pairing itself carries meaning, seeing your own twenty minutes next to the app's thirty five is what actually produces the moment of recognition, hiding the raw guess after the fact would quietly turn the feature from an honest mirror into a corrections notice.

Stage 3, confidence rendering rules. The confidence_level from the bias engine's output object maps to a specific visual treatment, not just a number in parentheses. Low confidence, 5 to 15 instances, renders the recalibrated number in a visually lighter or more muted style, with instance_count shown plainly, for example based on 6 sessions. Medium, 15 to 40, renders at full visual weight with instance_count still shown. High, 40 or more, renders at full visual weight and can drop the instance count to a smaller secondary label, since by that point the number has earned quiet authority rather than needing to keep justifying itself.

Stage 4, trend indicator. Alongside the number, a simple directional signal shows whether the recalibrated_estimate has moved up, down, or stayed flat over the last several recalculations, a small arrow or delta value, for example up from 32 minutes last month. This is a light read of the same drift data the bias engine already tracks, not a separate calculation, display just formats what the engine already knows into something glanceable.

Stage 5, fallback and cold start display. If confidence_level does not yet exist because the minimum instance threshold hasn't been met, the display shows only the raw guess field, no recalibrated number, no confidence badge, nothing implying data that doesn't exist yet. If the bias engine is showing a category level fallback rather than a specific context tag match, the display explicitly labels it as such, something like based on your work sessions generally, so the person knows this number is a broader estimate, not a precise one for this exact situation.

Stage 6, override behavior. If the person edits the pre-filled recalibrated number rather than accepting it, the override is accepted immediately with no confirmation step, consistent with the neutral, non-approval design running through the rest of the product. The override itself gets logged as a signal, not blocked or second-guessed, since a rising override rate across a category is itself useful information worth surfacing later, possibly meaning the recalibrated number has stopped matching reality for that person.

Shipping order: stages 1 through 3 form the minimum viable display, since a plain paired number with a basic confidence label already delivers the core insight. Stage 4, the trend arrow, is a natural short follow up once stage 3 exists, since it reuses the same underlying data. Stages 5 and 6 are correctness details that should be in place before wide release, since a cold start user seeing a fake-looking number, or an override that feels blocked, would both undermine trust in the product's core honesty early.

**Map and traffic integration.** Sits underneath the trip category. The app calls a routing API in the background to get a live, objective baseline time for any trip, which becomes one of the inputs the bias engine compares against the person's own guess and eventual actual outcome. This is infrastructure, not a feature the app is trying to own.

*Algorithm, staged rollout:*

Stage 1, baseline fetch on trip creation. The moment a trip is created as a session object, the app calls a routing API, Google Maps or an equivalent, with origin, destination, and current time, and stores the result as api_baseline_duration on that session, separate from both estimated_duration and actual_duration. This call happens once at creation, giving an objective, real time reference point the person's own guess can be compared against later, independent of whether the person even provides a guess of their own.

Stage 2, live refresh near departure. Since traffic conditions shift, a single fetch at creation time can go stale if the trip is planned well in advance. The app refetches the routing estimate shortly before the trip's expected start, a window like fifteen to thirty minutes prior, updating api_baseline_duration to reflect current traffic rather than the conditions from whenever the trip was first added. This refreshed number is what actually gets shown to the person alongside their own guess and the recalibrated estimate, not the original stale fetch.

Stage 3, three way comparison. Once a trip closes, the session carries three numbers together, the person's raw estimated_duration, the api_baseline_duration from the routing service, and the actual_duration from GPS tracking. This three way structure is what lets the bias engine learn something the routing service itself has no reason to model, whether this specific person tends to run later than the API's own prediction, not just whether the API was accurate that day. A person consistently arriving eight minutes after the API's estimate is a distinct, personal signal, separate from whether the baseline itself was good or bad.

Stage 4, rate limiting and cost control. Routing API calls typically cost money per request or count against a rate limit, so fetches are scoped deliberately, one call at trip creation, one refresh near departure, not a continuous poll while the trip is pending. If a person creates several trips in a batch, for instance planning a whole day at once, fetches queue and stage rather than firing all at once against the API.

Stage 5, fallback when the API is unavailable. If the routing call fails, times out, or the person is offline, api_baseline_duration is stored as null for that session rather than blocking trip creation or closing. The bias engine and display layer both already handle a null gracefully, falling back to relying on the person's own estimated_duration and actual_duration alone, the same two numbers the product would have if map integration didn't exist at all.

Shipping order: stage 1, the single fetch at creation, is the minimum viable version and should ship first, since even a stale baseline is a meaningful reference point. Stage 3, the three way comparison, activates automatically the moment stage 1 exists alongside the bias engine, no extra work needed beyond storing the extra field. Stage 2, the live refresh, is a real improvement worth adding once the basic version is proven, since it's the difference between a rough baseline and an actually trustworthy one. Stages 4 and 5 are operational safeguards that should be in place before this reaches any meaningful number of users, since API costs and outages are both certainties at scale, not edge cases.

**Personal insight view.** A place to see patterns over time, which categories someone is most wrong about, whether their estimates are improving, and the confidence trend behind each one.

*Algorithm, staged rollout:*

Stage 1, category ranking. Pull the bias engine's output object for every category and context tag pair a person has enough data on, and rank them by how far recalibrated_multiplier sits from 1, largest deviation first. This produces the single most useful list in the view, where you are most wrong, sorted plainly rather than alphabetically or by how often a category occurs.

Stage 2, trend over time per category. For each ranked category, render the recalibrated_multiplier's own history as a simple line, not just its current value, using the same underlying series the bias engine's drift detection already tracks. This shows whether a person's estimating is actually getting better, worse, or holding steady in that category, since a single current number can't show direction on its own.

Stage 3, aggregate accuracy score. Compute one overall summary figure, for example the average absolute deviation from 1 across all of a person's categories, weighted by instance_count so categories with more data count for more. This gives a single honest headline number for the view, something like your estimates are off by an average of eighteen percent right now, without pretending false precision in categories that barely have any data yet.

Stage 4, drift surfaced plainly. Any category currently carrying a drift_flag from the bias engine gets a visible marker in the view, not buried, since a real pattern shift, a new job, a new commute, deserves to be seen rather than quietly folded into a long running average the person never notices changed.

Stage 5, time range filtering. Let the person view the same ranking and trends over different windows, last thirty days versus all time, since a category might look bad over the full history but has actually been improving lately, and only a filterable range makes that visible rather than one fixed lifetime average hiding recent progress.

Shipping order: stage 1 is the minimum viable view, a ranked list already delivers real value on its own. Stage 2 and stage 3 should follow shortly after, since both draw on data the bias engine already produces and mainly require display work rather than new computation. Stage 4 and stage 5 are refinements worth adding once the core view is proven useful to real users.

**Procrastination nudge.** Since the app already knows a task tends to run longer than the person expects, it can nudge them to start earlier than planned, rather than only correcting the number after the fact.

*Algorithm, staged rollout:*

Stage 1, deadline aware trigger. For a task or trip carrying a deadline or a fixed start time it needs to align with, for instance a meeting to reach on time, compare the recalibrated_estimate against the time remaining before that fixed point. If recalibrated_estimate leaves less buffer than a configurable margin, for example less than fifteen minutes of slack once travel and setup are accounted for, the nudge condition is met.

Stage 2, single gentle prompt, not a repeating alarm. When the condition is met, the app surfaces one nudge, phrased plainly around the person's own history, for example your last ten sessions like this ran forty minutes, you might want to start now rather than in twenty. This fires once per instance rather than repeating or escalating, consistent with the product's non-nagging, non-judgmental tone established elsewhere.

Stage 3, confidence gate. The nudge only fires when confidence_level for that category and context tag is at least medium, since a nudge built on two shaky data points is more likely to annoy than help, and a wrong nudge early on would cost trust in every nudge that follows.

Stage 4, respecting context tags. The nudge pulls the recalibrated_multiplier for whatever context tag is active on this instance, not a blended category average, so a task tagged high pressure triggers off the high pressure specific number rather than a misleadingly optimistic general average.

Stage 5, dismissal as signal. If a person dismisses or ignores a nudge repeatedly for a given category, that dismissal rate is tracked quietly, and past some threshold the nudge frequency for that specific category tapers off automatically, rather than the app continuing to interrupt with something the person has clearly signaled they don't want.

Shipping order: stage 1 and 2 form the minimum viable nudge and should ship together, since a nudge with no gating logic behind it is just noise. Stage 3 is a near immediate follow on, since firing nudges on thin data actively damages trust rather than helping. Stage 4 activates naturally once context tags exist as their own feature. Stage 5 is a refinement worth adding once real usage shows how often people actually dismiss nudges in practice.

**Gap filler suggestions.** When there's a free window between two fixed events, the app actively suggests something that fits, drawn from the person's own tasks and routines, including personal habits like a recurring call with a specific person, timed the same way any other duration is tracked. Where the person has connected a planning tool they already use, Reminders, Notion, Todoist, or similar, the app also pulls in anything actually planned but not yet done and matches it against the size of the open window, so a fifteen minute gap surfaces the quick reminder that's been sitting untouched rather than only the app's own guess at a routine. This connection is opt in and reads only task names and due dates, nothing else in someone's notes. The feature does not rank downtime against productive activities as better or worse, it shows the factual time spent on whatever the person chooses and lets them decide if that's what they want.

*Algorithm, staged rollout:*

Stage 1, gap detection. Scan the person's fixed calendar events or logged sessions for the day, identify any open window between two of them above a minimum size worth suggesting into, for example ten minutes or more, and compute window_duration for each gap found.

Stage 2, candidate matching from personal history. Pull every category and context tag pair the person has meaningful history for, and filter to those whose recalibrated_estimate or typical range fits within window_duration, with some tolerance, for example a fit if the activity's usual duration is no more than the window size. Rank candidates by how often that activity actually occurs in the person's history, most frequent first, so the suggestion favors real habits over rarely logged one offs.

Stage 3, planning tool merge. Pull any outstanding, undone items from a connected planning tool, filtered to just name and due_date, and treat each as an additional candidate alongside the personal history candidates from stage 2. If an item's presence in the planning tool doesn't carry a known duration, since it was never logged as a session before, default to showing it as a candidate without a time estimate attached, letting the recalibration piece apply on its next occurrence once it's actually been done through the app once.

Stage 4, ranked suggestion set. Merge candidates from stage 2 and stage 3 into a single ranked list, capped at a small number shown at once, for example three, rather than presenting an overwhelming list for a short window. Planning tool items with an approaching due_date are weighted slightly higher in this merge, since a pending reminder about to become overdue carries more urgency than a purely optional routine.

Stage 5, neutral logging regardless of choice. Whatever the person actually chooses, including ignoring every suggestion and doing something else entirely, gets logged the same neutral way as any other session, contributing duration data back into that category's history. The suggestion engine never blocks or judges the eventual choice, it only ever offers options into the gap.

Shipping order: stage 1 and 2 form the minimum viable version, personal history alone already produces useful suggestions. Stage 4's ranking and capping should ship alongside, since an uncapped or unranked list undermines the whole point of a quick, low friction suggestion. Stage 3, the planning tool merge, is a valuable but separate integration that can follow once the core suggestion loop is working well on personal history alone. Stage 5 is a design constraint present from the start, not a later addition.

**Context tags on categories.** Not every occurrence of a task type is really the same situation. A normal work session and a high pressure, deadline driven one shouldn't blend into the same average. The person attaches a context tag at the moment they log the estimate, not afterward, since the whole point is separating these situations before the outcome happens, not explaining away a result once it's already in. The app starts everyone off with a small fixed set, normal, high pressure, low energy, distracted, so there's no setup required on day one, just a tap or a spoken word. People can also add their own tags beyond that set, since a fixed list will never cover everything, someone might need "kids are home" and no default option captures that. Once a custom tag shows up often enough in a person's own history, the app starts surfacing it as a suggestion alongside the defaults. Underneath, the bias engine tracks a separate multiplier per category per context, so a work session tagged high pressure and a work session tagged normal build their own honest averages instead of blurring into one number that doesn't describe either situation well. This is framed as context, not as an excuse or an override, normal is just one context among several, not the baseline everything else deviates from.

*Algorithm, staged rollout:*

Stage 1, default tag set at capture. At the moment of estimate capture, present the fixed default tags, normal, high pressure, low energy, distracted, as one tap or one spoken word options, with normal pre-selected as a neutral default rather than an implied correct state. context_tag is stored alongside category_id on the session object from the very first stage of estimate capture, not added on afterward.

Stage 2, custom tag creation. Allow free text entry of a new tag at the same capture moment, stored the same way as a default tag from that point forward, no structural difference between a default and a custom one once created.

Stage 3, custom tag promotion. Track usage_count per custom tag per person. Once a custom tag's usage_count crosses a threshold, for example used three or more times, promote it into the person's own quick select list alongside the defaults, so it appears as a tap option rather than requiring free text entry every time going forward.

Stage 4, per tag dataset isolation. The bias engine, as already defined, keys every calculation by category_id and context_tag together. This feature's whole job is making sure that pairing is populated correctly and early, at the point of capture, so the engine never has to guess or infer context after the fact.

Stage 5, tag level fallback. If a specific context_tag has too few instances to be trusted on its own, the display and bias engine already fall back to the category level number, per the bias engine's own stage 5, labeled clearly as broader rather than precise, so a brand new tag never blocks the person from seeing a usable number while it accumulates its own history.

Shipping order: stage 1, the fixed default set, is the minimum viable version and must exist from the very first release, since it defines the data shape everything else depends on. Stage 2, custom tags, should ship alongside or immediately after, since the fixed set alone will always miss real situations. Stage 3, promotion into quick select, is a meaningful usability improvement worth adding once there's enough real usage to know which custom tags people actually repeat. Stage 4 and 5 are properties that fall out of the bias engine's own design rather than separate work, confirming this feature and that one stay in sync.

**Progress based gamification.** Built around time saved or speed of completion, not a plain streak counter, since rewarding the outcome, actually getting better at planning, matters more than rewarding the habit of opening the app. This needs careful design, since rewarding "less time wasted" risks contradicting the app's neutral stance on how people choose to spend their time, and that tension has to be resolved deliberately rather than accidentally.

*Algorithm, staged rollout:*

Stage 1, define the scored quantity carefully. The score is never based on how someone chose to spend a gap, downtime and productive time are never weighted differently, only on estimation accuracy itself, meaning how close a person's own guess or the app's recalibrated number came to the eventual actual_duration. This keeps the score aligned with what the product actually measures, planning skill, rather than sliding into judging how someone lives.

Stage 2, per instance accuracy score. For each closed session, compute an accuracy_score based on how close bias_ratio sits to 1, the closer to 1 the better the score, regardless of whether the session ran long or short, since both directions represent an equally real estimation gap.

Stage 3, rolling personal accuracy trend. Aggregate accuracy_score over a rolling window, for example the last thirty days, per category, and surface whether it's trending toward 1 over time, meaning the person's own estimating is measurably improving. This is the core positive signal the gamification layer is built around, not any single instance being good or bad.

Stage 4, milestone framing over streaks. Rather than a daily streak that breaks and resets, use milestone language tied to real improvement, for example your commute estimates have been within ten percent of actual for the last two weeks. This rewards genuine progress and survives a missed day gracefully, instead of punishing a skipped day the way a broken streak counter would.

Stage 5, opt out entirely. Since this is the one feature most likely to conflict with the product's neutral, non-judging design if built carelessly, it needs to be fully optional, visible only if the person chooses to turn it on, off by default until proven it adds real value rather than pressure.

Shipping order: this feature should ship last among the core list, after the bias engine, display, and insight view are all working well, since it depends entirely on their data and adds real design risk if rushed. Stage 1 and 2 are the necessary foundation. Stage 3 and 4 are the actual user facing version worth testing carefully with real people before wide release. Stage 5, the opt out, must be present from the first version of this feature, not added later.

**Voice input.** Natural spoken commands to start and end anything, saying something like "I'm starting a work session, guessing three hours" or "ending the work session," so an estimate and its actual outcome get logged without ever opening the app, typing, or touching the phone at all. This needs to actually work hands free, through Siri rather than only after tapping a microphone button, and it needs to understand more than one language, since plenty of people naturally think and speak in more than one depending on who they're talking to or what they're doing.

*Algorithm, staged rollout:*

Stage 1, intent parsing. Take the raw spoken phrase and extract three things, an intent, start or end, a category reference, matched against the person's existing category_id list, and optionally a duration if the phrase includes one. This is the one part of the whole system suited to a language model rather than fixed rules, since natural phrasing varies too much for a rigid parser to hold up.

Stage 2, category matching with confirmation on ambiguity. Match the spoken category reference against the person's existing categories using fuzzy matching, not exact string matching, since people won't always say a category name the same way twice. If the match confidence is low, or two categories seem equally likely, ask a single short clarifying question rather than guessing silently and risking a session logged under the wrong category.

Stage 3, duration extraction and normalization. If a duration is present in the phrase, parse it into the same integer minutes format estimate capture uses elsewhere, so voice, tap, and widget all write into one identical schema downstream. If no duration is spoken, proceed exactly as stage 1 of estimate capture handles a skipped guess, leaving estimated_duration null rather than blocking.

Stage 4, context tag extraction, optional. If the spoken phrase includes a recognizable context cue, for example mentioning a deadline or naming stress, attempt to map it to an existing context tag, defaulting to normal if nothing specific is detected or extracted, consistent with context tags always requiring an explicit value rather than staying blank.

Stage 5, end command matching to the correct open session. On an end phrase, match against the person's currently open sessions by category, closing the correct one even if multiple sessions happen to be open at once, rather than assuming only one session is ever active. If more than one open session plausibly matches, ask a short clarifying question rather than guessing which one to close.

Stage 6, hands-free activation through Siri. iOS doesn't let a third party app listen for its own custom wake word in the background, Siri is the one system level layer with that privilege, so hands-free activation runs through Apple's App Intents and App Shortcuts rather than any custom listener built from scratch. Starting or ending a session becomes a defined intent Siri can trigger directly, "Hey Siri, start a work session in Actual," with Apple's own wake word detection and first pass transcription handling the initial step before Actual's own parsing ever runs. A manual push to talk option, a microphone button on the widget, the lock screen, the watch, or the Mac menu bar, stays available as the fallback for whenever a phrase doesn't fit neatly into how Siri expects to hear it.

Stage 7, language selection. The person picks which languages they actually speak from a short list during setup, defaulting to whatever's already active in their phone's own keyboard and Siri settings rather than asking them to configure something from nothing. Apple's on device Speech framework needs to know which locale it's listening for, it doesn't reliably guess a language on its own, so with more than one language selected, the app tries whichever was used most recently first and falls back through the others rather than guessing blind on every attempt.

Stage 8, layered parsing per language. Apple's on device Foundation Models framework doesn't necessarily support every language equally well, its coverage has rolled out gradually since launch, so parsing runs in two layers. Where the on device model supports the language being spoken, it handles the same flexible, natural phrasing already described in stage 1. Where it doesn't, or that isn't confirmed, a simpler backup parser built from a fixed set of keywords per language, starting, ending, guessing, numbers, common time words, handles the same basic commands with less flexibility but a real guarantee that they work at all. This needs checking against Apple's current language support at build time rather than assumed once and forgotten, since that list keeps expanding.

Stage 9, category matching stays language agnostic regardless. Since category names come from whatever the person actually typed or said when creating them, in whatever language that was, matching a spoken phrase against the person's own category list never depends on translation, a category named in Russian matches a Russian phrase the same fuzzy way an English category matches an English one. Only the surrounding grammar, the words for starting, guessing, and the numbers themselves, needs language specific handling, the categories themselves take care of themselves.

Stage 10, a brief transcription check, not an approval step. Right after a command is parsed, whatever was actually heard shows briefly, started, work session, normal, guessed two hours, for a few seconds with a quick way to undo or correct it before it's treated as committed. This is not the estimate or outcome approval step ruled out earlier, it's purely a check against Actual having misheard something in background noise or an unfamiliar accent, catching a clearly wrong transcription is a different thing entirely from being asked to agree with a number the app already heard correctly.

Shipping order: stage 1 through 3 form the minimum viable version, parsing intent, category, and duration into the existing schema, for whichever single language ships first. Stage 4 can follow shortly after, once context tags exist as their own feature to map into. Stage 5 becomes necessary the moment more than one category can realistically be active at once, which is likely from fairly early on, so it shouldn't be deferred too far past initial release. Stage 6, Siri activation, is worth building alongside this very first version rather than after, since a voice feature that still needs the phone unlocked and tapped first isn't really hands free at all. Stages 7 through 9, language selection and layered parsing, are the real work behind supporting more than one language and should ship together as their own deliberate phase once the single language version is solid, rather than bolted on piecemeal language by language. Stage 10 is a small but real safeguard worth having from the start, since noise driven misfires are the fastest way to make a hands free feature feel untrustworthy.

**Home screen and lock screen widgets.** A running timer or countdown for whatever session is currently active, plus a one tap button to start or end something, all reachable without unlocking the phone, glancing at the lock screen, or opening the app at all.

*Algorithm, staged rollout:*

Stage 1, static quick start widget. A small set of the person's most frequently used category and context tag pairs, ranked by instance_count, rendered as one tap start buttons directly on the widget. Tapping one immediately creates a session with that category and context pre-filled, using whatever pre-fill logic estimate capture's stage 3 already defines, no app opening required.

Stage 2, active session display. When a session is currently open, the widget switches from showing quick start options to showing a live running timer for that session, along with the recalibrated_estimate for reference, so the person can glance at their lock screen or home screen and see both how long they've been going and how long this usually takes them.

Stage 3, one tap end. The same widget surface includes an end button while a session is active, closing the session immediately through the same logic actual outcome tracking's stage 3 already defines, no correction step, no confirmation, actual_duration computed and written the moment it's tapped.

Stage 4, widget refresh cadence. The live timer needs to update on a reasonable interval without draining battery, a periodic refresh, for example every minute, rather than a continuous live tick that most widget frameworks can't sustain efficiently anyway. This is a real platform constraint worth deciding early rather than assuming true live updating is free.

Stage 5, ranking refresh. The set of quick start options shown in stage 1 isn't fixed once and forgotten, it recalculates periodically based on updated instance_count and recency, so the widget surfaces whatever categories are actually most active in someone's current life rather than staying frozen on whatever was true during onboarding.

Stage 6, lock screen and Live Activity surface. Once a session is active, the same running timer and recalibrated_estimate also render as a Live Activity, the mechanism iOS provides for live, updating information directly on the lock screen and in the Dynamic Island on newer phones, so a person can see how long they've been going without unlocking the phone or even glancing at a widget. The one tap end button carries over onto this surface too, closing the session through the exact same logic already defined, no separate code path just because the surface is smaller and more visible.

Shipping order: stage 1 and 2 form the minimum viable widget, quick start and active session display are both needed for the widget to feel complete on day one rather than half finished. Stage 3 ships alongside, since a widget that starts something but can't end it is only half useful. Stage 6, the lock screen and Live Activity surface, is worth shipping in this same first release rather than after, since it reuses the exact same running session data and is often the very first thing someone sees without unlocking their phone at all. Stage 4 is a technical constraint to solve during initial build, not deferred. Stage 5 is a refinement worth adding once there's enough real usage to know the ranking is worth recalculating on.

**App and device usage tracking, secondary.** Most of what this app knows about someone's day has to come from the person themselves, saying out loud that they're making breakfast, going for a run, cleaning the flat, calling a specific person, all of that stays manual by design, since that's where the real substance of a day actually lives and no phone sensor can see it. This feature covers one narrow exception, the handful of screen time categories, scrolling TikTok, Instagram, and similar, that people are the least likely to log honestly on their own, precisely because they'd rather not admit to themselves how long it took. For that narrow slice only, the app can passively notice that an app was opened and when it closed, and feed that duration into the bias engine automatically. A companion app on macOS extends the exact same narrow idea to a laptop, noticing what application was open and for how long. It stops there. The app never knows what was actually happening inside that app, no content, no messages, no screenshots, no browsing history, just the fact that it was open and for how long, nothing more. Since even that narrow amount of visibility can start to feel like surveillance if it isn't built carefully, a few things are non negotiable. The raw record of what was open and when is processed on the device itself, only the resulting duration per category syncs to the person's account. Tracking is opt in per app, not a blanket switch. Whenever it's active, there is a persistent, honest indicator visible to the person, never something running silently in the background. This whole feature is a convenience for one specific blind spot, not the backbone of the product, everything else on this list keeps working perfectly well through the person's own voice and manual input alone.

*Algorithm, staged rollout:*

Stage 1, opt in scope selection. During setup, present a short, explicit list of apps or app categories eligible for passive tracking, defaulting to nothing selected. The person actively chooses which apps this applies to, there is no blanket monitoring switch and no pre-selected list of apps assumed to be a problem on the person's behalf.

Stage 2, on device open and close detection. For each opted in app, use the platform's own screen time or app usage framework, on iOS and Android respectively, to detect when that specific app moves to the foreground and background, computing open_duration entirely on device. No raw activity log, no timestamped list of every app switch, leaves the device, only an aggregated open_duration per app per day gets written locally.

Stage 3, category mapping and sync. Map each opted in app to an existing category_id, for example Instagram and TikTok both mapping to a broader scrolling or social category if the person prefers one combined bucket, or kept separate if they want granularity. Only the resulting duration per category, not per individual app open and close event, syncs to the person's account, feeding into the bias engine the same way any manually logged session would.

Stage 4, persistent visible indicator. Whenever passive tracking is active for any opted in app, a persistent, unobtrusive indicator is visible somewhere the person will actually see it, a permanent line in the app's own home screen rather than something buried three menus deep, so the tracking is never silent or easy to forget is happening.

Stage 5, macOS companion, same constraints. The macOS companion app applies stages 1 through 4 identically to desktop applications, using the operating system's own active application tracking rather than deep content inspection, syncing the same aggregated duration only, under the same opt in and visible indicator rules. It's built as a separate small application specifically because it targets a different operating system, but shares the same data contract and the same account as the phone app.

Shipping order: this entire feature ships after the core product is proven, since it is explicitly secondary. Stage 1 and 2 are the minimum viable version and must ship together, since opt in scope with no on device detection is pointless and on device detection without opt in scope is the exact surveillance framing to avoid. Stage 3 and 4 are non negotiable parts of the same initial release, not later additions, given how central trust is to this specific feature. Stage 5, the macOS companion, is reasonably built as its own follow-on release once the phone version is working and trusted.

**Shared reminder links.** Someone outside the app can hand a person a reminder without either of them needing to already share the app together. Sam wants a call sometime Saturday and thinks it will take about twenty five minutes, so instead of hoping Maksym remembers on his own, Sam sends a link that opens straight into Actual on Maksym's phone, showing what Sam asked for, the window it's meant to happen in, and Sam's own rough guess at the length. The moment Maksym actually has a free stretch that fits that guess, say an hour open with nothing else on, the app asks him directly whether he wants to do it now, rather than leaving it sitting quietly in a list for him to notice on his own. From there it behaves like anything else he logs himself, a guess is captured, the real duration gets timed the same honest way as a work session or a commute, and it becomes one more instance in his own history. Sam never sees how long the call actually took, only that it happened, and only if Maksym chooses to let him know that much.

*Algorithm, staged rollout:*

Stage 1, link generation. Any task or reminder object can be marked shareable. Marking it generates a share_id, a short random token bound to {title, window_start, window_end, sender_id, created_at, sender_estimated_duration}. sender_estimated_duration is optional and comes straight from the sender typing how long they think it will take, Sam guessing twenty five minutes for his call being the example. No category_id or context_tag rides along with it, since those describe the recipient's own relationship to the task, not the sender's, and get chosen later by whoever actually receives it. The window can be a fixed range or something looser like anytime Saturday, stored as the full day rather than a specific slot.

Stage 2, deep link resolution. Opening the link on a phone with the app already installed and signed in drops straight into a reminder preview screen showing the sender's name, the title, and the window. Opening it without the app installed lands on a plain web page carrying the same preview, with the share_id held in a pending state so it resolves into the app automatically once installed, a standard deferred deep link pattern rather than a second manual step.

Stage 3, acceptance and local mapping. On accept, the recipient assigns their own category_id and, optionally, a context_tag, exactly as they would creating any manual task, the sender's title becomes a suggested label, not a locked category. estimated_duration stays null at this point, same as stage 1 of estimate capture, since the recipient hasn't guessed anything yet. The result is an ordinary session object, carrying a window_deadline instead of a start_time until the recipient actually begins it.

Stage 4, fit triggered prompt, not silent listing. Rather than sitting passively in a suggestions list waiting to be noticed, the shared reminder only surfaces the moment gap detection finds a free window inside the deadline that's genuinely big enough for it, comparing window_duration against sender_estimated_duration when one was given, or a sensible default size when it wasn't. Maksym having an hour free with nothing else on, and Sam's own guess of twenty five minutes fitting comfortably inside that, is exactly the moment the app steps in and asks directly whether he wants to do it now, rather than leaving him to spot it himself in a list. If no fitting window has turned up and the deadline is getting close, a single fallback prompt still fires before the window closes, but the fit based version above is the primary trigger, not a passive backup plan.

Stage 5, completion through the existing loop. Once Maksym answers the prompt and starts it, it runs through the same estimate capture and actual outcome tracking flow as anything else. If he already has history for this category and context tag, the usual recalibrated number pre-fills the guess exactly as it always does. If this is the first time, sender_estimated_duration pre-fills it instead, clearly labeled as Sam's guess rather than his own history, so there's still a sensible starting number even with nothing logged yet. Either way the real duration gets logged on close, and this is the point where the shared reminder stops being a special object and simply becomes one more instance in the recipient's own history for whichever category he mapped it to.

Stage 6, optional completion ping. If the recipient allows it, off by default, the sender gets a single boolean notification once the item is marked done, nothing about duration, guess, or timing, only that it happened. The recipient's own numbers stay private, since the product's whole premise is that this data belongs to whoever logged it, not whoever asked for it.

Stage 7, expiry. A reminder whose window closes without ever being acted on gets marked expired rather than deleted, staying visible in the recipient's queue as a plain, undone item, similar to how a flagged low confidence session stays visible elsewhere rather than vanishing. Nothing about this carries judgment, it's a factual record that this one didn't happen.

Shipping order: stage 1 through 3 form the minimum viable version, generate a link, receive it, accept it into a normal session, and this now includes carrying the sender's own estimate along with it, since it costs nothing extra to store. Stage 4, the fit based prompt, needs to exist before this feature is genuinely useful, since without it a shared reminder is just another item sitting quietly in a list, which is exactly the passive experience worth avoiding here. Stage 5's use of sender_estimated_duration as a cold start pre-fill follows naturally once stage 4 exists. Stage 6 is optional by design and can ship later without weakening the core loop. Stage 7 is a small edge case addition worth having before wide release.

**Quick start, categorized after.** Sometimes there's no moment to stop and pick a category before starting, someone just needs to hit start. This lets that happen, pick an expected time, start the timer immediately, and the one thing that has to happen before the session really counts is a quick label right when it ends, work, gym, whatever it turns out to have been. The friction lands at the one point where it costs almost nothing, right after the person already knows what they just did, instead of making them stop and think before they've even started.

*Algorithm, staged rollout:*

Stage 1, blind start object. From any quick start surface, a widget, the lock screen, the watch, or a desktop menu bar, tapping start creates a session object with only estimated_duration and start_time populated. category_id and context_tag are both left null, no prompt for either is shown at this point, since the whole purpose of this path is skipping that step until the moment the person actually knows what they're logging.

Stage 2, expected time captured at the same moment. The one thing still asked for at start is the expected duration, using the same quick preset chips or a spoken number the regular capture flow already uses, since that number is what the whole comparison depends on and costs almost nothing to give even in a rush.

Stage 3, mandatory resolution at end. Ending the session, from any surface, immediately opens a short "what was this" prompt before the session counts as closed, a handful of the person's most frequent categories shown as one tap chips, plus a way to type something new. actual_duration is computed and stored the moment the timer stops regardless, but the session stays pending, excluded from the bias engine, until category_id is chosen.

Stage 4, context tag at the same resolution step. Context tag is asked for alongside category at this same moment, defaulting to normal if skipped, consistent with context tags always needing an explicit value. Since this happens the instant the session ends, before the person has moved on to whatever's next, it stays close enough to the moment that it doesn't reopen the exact risk context tags were built to avoid, picking a tag well after the fact specifically to explain away a result.

Stage 5, no indefinite backlog. If the resolution prompt gets dismissed without a choice, the session stays visible as unresolved in a small, plainly visible list, not buried, and a single follow up prompt appears the next time the app is opened for any reason, rather than repeating on a timer. This isn't meant to be a queue people let build up, an unresolved session contributes nothing to their own history until it's resolved, so letting these pile up quietly defeats the point of quick start in the first place.

Stage 6, once resolved, joins the same pipeline. The moment category_id and context_tag are set, the session is treated exactly like any other closed session from that point forward, feeding the bias engine, the insight view, everything else, with no distinction left between a session categorized at the start and one categorized a minute after it ended.

Shipping order: stage 1 and 2 form the minimum viable version, since blind start and expected time is the whole point of the feature. Stage 3 has to ship alongside, not after, since a quick start with no resolution step is exactly the ungoverned backlog worth avoiding. Stage 4 rides along with stage 3 for free. Stage 5 is a small but real safeguard worth having before wide release, since without it the unresolved pile becomes its own quiet failure mode. Stage 6 isn't separate engineering work, it falls out naturally once stage 3 exists.

**Cross-device sync, private by design.** Every device Actual runs on, phone, watch, Mac, needs to see the same running sessions and the same history, but none of that has to pass through a server Actual itself operates. The sync layer sits directly on each person's own private iCloud account, so their data only ever exists on devices signed into their own Apple ID, with a faster direct channel between phone and watch when they happen to be near each other, and nothing else standing in between.

*Algorithm, staged rollout:*

Stage 1, private container as the source of truth. Every session object, category, context tag, and bias engine output lives in the person's own private CloudKit database, a per user, encrypted iCloud container that only their own signed in devices can read or write, with no equivalent copy stored on any server Actual operates. Whichever device makes a change, phone, watch, or Mac, is really just writing to this one shared private store.

Stage 2, direct phone to watch channel. Between a paired iPhone and its Apple Watch, most changes travel over Apple's own WatchConnectivity framework, a direct Bluetooth or local Wi-Fi link that skips iCloud entirely, since the two devices are usually near each other and this path is faster and doesn't depend on wider network reachability. A session started on the watch reaches the phone almost immediately through this, with iCloud kept as the eventual record for everything else, the Mac included.

Stage 3, iCloud fallback and Mac reach. Anything the direct phone to watch link doesn't cover, a session logged from the Mac, or the watch and phone catching back up after being apart, syncs through the private iCloud container itself rather than any direct connection, since there's no equivalent close range channel between a Mac and either the phone or the watch.

Stage 4, conflict resolution. Since more than one device can log something while offline from the others, a work session ended on the Mac while the phone happened to be asleep for instance, CloudKit's own per record versioning resolves which write wins on reconnect, defaulting to whichever change is more recent, with a genuine conflict, two different sessions closed under the same category at overlapping times, surfaced quietly in the insight view as worth a second look rather than silently discarded.

Stage 5, what Android support would actually require. None of the private container or the direct watch channel described above exists outside Apple's own ecosystem, so shipping on Android later isn't a port of this sync layer, it's a second one built from entirely different pieces. An Android build would need its own sync backend, most realistically a server Actual does operate at that point, or a comparable per user encrypted store on a platform like Firebase, since Android has no built in equivalent of a private iCloud container. It would also need its own direct phone to watch equivalent for Wear OS, using Google's own on device data layer API rather than WatchConnectivity, and a clear answer for whether an iPhone and an Android phone could ever share one account at all, since the entire privacy pitch up to that point rests on nothing living outside Apple's own private storage, and any company operated server changes that pitch, even one that only ever touches encrypted data.

Shipping order: stage 1 through 3 are the whole sync layer for the Apple only version of the product and need to ship together, since a partial version of this, iCloud without the faster watch channel, or the reverse, leaves an obviously worse experience on one device. Stage 4 is a real requirement from day one as well, not an edge case, since any sync system without conflict handling eventually corrupts someone's own history. Stage 5 isn't a build task right now at all, it's a decision to make later, whether Android means a second, differently built sync layer under the same privacy promise, or a genuine change to that promise, and that decision is worth making deliberately rather than backing into it once Android is already half built.

**Apple Watch companion.** A watch app scoped to the handful of things worth doing from the wrist, starting something, ending it, and glancing at whatever's currently running, without pulling out the phone at all. It reuses the exact same quick start and voice logic already documented rather than trying to fit the whole app onto a screen that small.

*Algorithm, staged rollout:*

Stage 1, complication and quick start. A watch face complication shows either a short list of the person's most frequent categories as tap targets, or, while nothing is running, a plain start button. Tapping either immediately creates a quick start session the same way quick start's stage 1 already does, expected duration entered through the digital crown or a spoken word before the timer begins.

Stage 2, live glance while active. While a session is running, the watch face and the app's own screen both show the live timer and the recalibrated_estimate for that category if one exists, syncing from the same session object the phone would show, not a separate state the watch keeps on its own.

Stage 3, one tap end plus voice. Ending from the watch works the same one tap way actual outcome tracking's stage 3 already defines, or through the watch's own microphone using the same voice parsing logic documented for voice input, "ending work session," rather than a separate, smaller version of that feature built just for the watch.

Stage 4, resolution on whichever device is closest at hand. Since quick start's mandatory category resolution has to happen right when a session ends, and ending will often happen on the watch itself, those same resolution chips render right there on the watch screen too, not only on the phone, since sending someone back to their phone just to answer a one tap question would defeat the entire point of running this from the wrist.

Stage 5, sync through the private container, not a separate account. The watch app holds no data of its own beyond what it needs to render the current state, every session it starts, updates, or ends writes into the same private iCloud container the phone app uses, reaching the phone almost immediately through the direct phone to watch channel described under cross-device sync, so a person's history looks no different depending on which device happened to log it.

Shipping order: stage 1 through 3 form the minimum viable watch app, start, glance, end. Stage 4 has to ship alongside stage 3, since an end button with nowhere to resolve the category is a dead end on this surface specifically. Stage 5 is a foundational sync requirement present from the first version, not layered on later, since a watch app that quietly drifted from the phone's own record would be worse than not having one at all.

**macOS menu bar companion.** For work sessions and other things that happen at a computer, pulling out a phone or lifting a wrist is often the least natural moment to start a timer. This adds Actual to the Mac's own menu bar, a persistent icon showing the running timer when something is active, and a one click way to start, expected time and all, without switching to a phone at any point. This is a different job from the macOS companion described earlier that quietly measures which applications are open, though it's reasonable for the same installed Mac app to do both, one passive and always on, one active only when someone deliberately starts something.

*Algorithm, staged rollout:*

Stage 1, menu bar quick start. Clicking the menu bar icon while nothing is running shows the same short list of frequent categories and plain start option quick start's stage 1 already defines, expected duration entered through a small dropdown or typed number, no separate window or full app launch required.

Stage 2, persistent running state. Once started, the menu bar icon itself changes to show a live, ticking duration, glanceable without opening any menu at all, the same running session object the phone or watch app would show if either were open at the same time.

Stage 3, one click end and resolution. Clicking end opens the same short "what was this" resolution step quick start's stage 3 defines, rendered as a small dropdown right from the menu bar rather than a full window, since the whole point of this surface is staying out of the way until the two moments that actually need input, starting and resolving.

Stage 4, keyboard shortcut. A configurable global keyboard shortcut triggers start and end without touching the mouse at all, useful for someone who wants to log a work session the instant they sit down without breaking their hands away from the keyboard.

Stage 5, shared private container, same as the watch. Exactly like the watch app, this holds no separate data of its own, every session writes into the same private iCloud container and feeds the same bias engine, regardless of whether it started from a phone, a watch, or a Mac's menu bar, reaching the others through the sync layer described under cross-device sync rather than any server Actual operates.

Shipping order: stage 1 through 3 form the minimum viable version. Stage 4, the keyboard shortcut, is a natural, low cost addition once start and end already exist. Stage 5 is a constraint present from day one, not added later, for the same reason it matters on the watch.

## What is deliberately out of scope for now

Money and cost estimation was part of the original concept and follows the exact same loop, a guess against a category, an actual outcome, a personal multiplier. It is left out of this version because it introduces bank connections, manual receipt entry, and financial data security concerns that have nothing to do with proving the core time based insight. It can be added later as its own item once the time side is working and people are using it daily.

Public transport ticket purchasing and full route planning are also left out. These require city by city API access from local transit authorities, a slow business development problem, not a product problem, and they don't differentiate the app from Citymapper or Google Maps, which already do this well in many cities.

## Hypothetical user experience

Marta commutes to an office three mornings a week and works from home the rest. She opens the app for the first time and adds her regular commute as a trip. The app asks how long she thinks it will take, she says twenty five minutes, the number she's always assumed since the drive looks short on a map. In the background, the app quietly pulls a live routing estimate for that same trip and stores it alongside her guess.

She drives in. The app tracks her real departure and arrival automatically through GPS. The actual time was forty minutes. Nothing prompts her to explain why, no pop up asks if today was unusual, it just gets logged as one data point in her commute category.

A few weeks in, she's driven the same commute a dozen times, sometimes thirty minutes, sometimes forty five. The next time she opens the app to log the trip, instead of just her own field for a guess, she now sees a second number next to it, based on her last twelve trips, this one usually runs closer to thirty eight minutes, with a small note that this is based on twelve logged trips rather than two, so she can trust it's a real pattern and not a fluke.

One Tuesday, she says out loud to her phone, "starting my work session, guessing two hours," and ends it later with "ending work session," never opening the app either time. That evening she flips to her insight view and sees her work sessions have been running closer to two hours forty across the last month, a gap she genuinely hadn't noticed until it was written down plainly in front of her.

Later that week she has ninety minutes free between finishing a call and picking up her kid. The app knows from her own history that a call with her mother usually runs about fifteen minutes and that she has a recurring habit of reading for around twenty, so it suggests both as options that would fit the window, alongside the honest note that she's spent an average of thirty minutes on her phone in similar gaps over the past month. She picks up the phone and calls her mother. The app logs it the same neutral way it logs everything else, no better, no worse, just what happened.

Over time, the numbers she sees before starting something get quietly more accurate, not because the app is telling her what to do, but because it has been watching her own patterns long enough to know her better than her own first guess does.

## Technology stack

Apple only for now, iPhone, Apple Watch, and Mac, on the reasoning already covered under cross-device sync. Everything below is the actual set of frameworks that combination points to.

**Language and app framework.** Swift and SwiftUI across every target, iPhone, Apple Watch, and Mac, from one shared codebase, since SwiftUI is the only framework that reaches all three without rebuilding the same screens two or three times over.

**Data and sync.** SwiftData paired with CloudKit, saving locally and mirroring automatically into the person's own private iCloud container, no server Actual operates anywhere in this loop. Core Data with CloudKit is the older, more battle tested fallback if SwiftData's newer edges cause real trouble during the build.

**Phone to watch.** WatchConnectivity for the direct, low latency channel between a paired iPhone and its Apple Watch, with iCloud kept as the fallback for anything that channel doesn't cover.

**Widgets and lock screen.** WidgetKit for the home screen widget, ActivityKit for the Live Activity that shows the running timer on the lock screen and in the Dynamic Island.

**Mac menu bar.** SwiftUI's MenuBarExtra, available from macOS 13 onward, for the persistent icon, dropdown, and keyboard shortcut described under the macOS menu bar companion.

**Voice and language parsing.** Apple's Speech framework for on device transcription, paired with the Foundation Models framework, Apple's own on device language model shipped as part of Apple Intelligence, for the fuzzy category and duration parsing voice input needs. Both stay entirely on the phone, nothing gets sent to any server.

**Charts.** Swift Charts for every trend line and ranked bar in the insight view, native, and it already matches dark mode and system text sizing without extra work.

**Maps and routing.** MapKit and its directions API for the live baseline time trip categories need, kept inside Apple's own ecosystem rather than adding Google as a second vendor and a second billing account.

**Reminder sharing.** CKShare, CloudKit's own record sharing mechanism, for handing a reminder from one iCloud user to another without any custom backend, plus one small static web page and a single serverless function for the fallback case, someone opening the link before the app is installed.

**Testing.** Swift Testing, or XCTest on an older Xcode, aimed first at the bias engine's own math, since it's pure logic with clear expected inputs and outputs and is the single most valuable place in the whole app to have real, rigorous tests early.

**Keeping the core logic clean.** The bias engine, the session lifecycle, and the recalibration math should live in their own plain Swift files with no SwiftUI, SwiftData, or CloudKit imports mixed in, taking numbers in and handing numbers back, nothing else. Costs nothing extra now and it's the one piece of real insurance against a future platform rewrite.

**Toolchain.** Xcode, on a Mac, since none of the above compiles, runs, or ships anywhere else. Confirm the minimum iOS and macOS versions against Apple's current documentation before setting deployment targets, since Live Activities and the Foundation Models framework both depend on fairly recent OS versions and that baseline moves over time.
